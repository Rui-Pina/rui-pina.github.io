My political positions rest on three pillars: **Monarchism**, **Corporatism**, and **Crime-Focused Deterrence**. Below I lay out the reasoning behind each.

## Monarchism

Monarchism is one of the most misunderstood political systems in the world. It is often dismissed as a relic of the Middle Ages, an image reinforced by cartoons and films of a king hoarding wealth on a golden throne while his people starve. That caricature does not survive contact with the facts.

### It is not a drain on the taxpayer

The claim that monarchies are simply too expensive, and that all tax money flows to one man to spend as he pleases, does not hold up. Take the United Kingdom: the government spends around £100 million a year to maintain the royal family, roughly £4.5 per citizen. What is rarely mentioned is that the Crown Estate, privately held by the royal family, earns around £312 million a year. Since the reign of George III, every monarch has voluntarily surrendered these profits to the government in exchange for a fixed annual sum, meaning the UK nets around £200 million a year *because* of the monarchy. If the Crown Estate were privatized instead, the state would only capture a fraction of that revenue through corporate tax; the rest would go to private shareholders. The monarchy, as it stands, effectively lowers the tax burden on ordinary citizens. The Habsburgs are another example: by the 19th century, only 0.5–1% of the state budget went to maintaining the imperial family, comparable to the cost of building new roads.

### Owners think generations ahead; renters do not

A monarch must think in terms of his own lifespan and that of his heirs. Because the state is, in effect, his family's property, he has every incentive to preserve and grow it rather than extract short-term value from it. A democratic leader, by contrast, is a *renter* of the state, not an owner. With no stake beyond the next election, the incentive is to spend as much as possible, as quickly as possible, and let the next occupant deal with the consequences. This asymmetry of incentives explains much of the reckless spending and short-termism endemic to modern republics.

### No system of acquiring power is perfectly fair

Critics ask why someone should hold power simply by virtue of birth. But no method of acquiring high office is free of arbitrariness. Elected leaders reach power through campaigning, rhetoric, alliances, and often corruption, and their success owes as much to persuasion and circumstance as to merit. An heir, by contrast, does not need to campaign or lie to obtain the position: it is settled from birth, leaving the whole of his upbringing free to be devoted to education and preparation for rule. This also removes the instability of contested succession: no one can rig who becomes the next monarch, whereas the outcome of the next election, or the one after that, is never guaranteed. Inheritance of position is, moreover, not unique to monarchy. Farms and family businesses are passed from parent to child as a matter of course. If one can inherit land, wealth, or a business, there is no principled reason inheriting a position of leadership should be uniquely objectionable.

### Monarchy and democracy are not opposites

Monarchism is frequently conflated with the absence of democracy, but the two are not mutually exclusive. Democracy is a system in which people elect their representatives; nothing about that requires the absence of a hereditary head of state. By the turn of the 20th century, most European monarchies had already become constitutional, with citizens electing prime ministers, parliaments, and local government, while the monarch remained a neutral, non-partisan figure above party politics. Meanwhile, many 20th-century republics, the USSR, Yugoslavia, and others, were considerably more authoritarian than the constitutional monarchies of the same era. Republic and democracy are not synonyms, and monarchy and democracy are not opposites. The first recorded constitutional monarchy is the Hittite Empire, dating back to roughly 1500 BC, where the king ruled alongside officials with independent authority.

### It is not an obsolete system

Today, 43 countries retain some form of monarchy, and five of the ten countries with the highest quality of life in the world are monarchies. If age alone made a system obsolete, democracy, also practiced since antiquity, would be equally obsolete. What matters is whether a system has adapted, and constitutional monarchies plainly have: modern monarchs no longer claim to rule by divine right but operate under a social contract, acting as a unifying, non-partisan voice above the party politics of the day.

### The monarch has the least incentive to be corrupt

Corruption is driven above all by the pursuit of money and power. A monarch is born already in possession of both, and holds them for life, removing the primary incentive to seek them illegitimately. Politicians, by contrast, hold office only temporarily and have every incentive to extract as much wealth and influence as they can before their term ends, a dynamic worsened by lobbying, where organized interests pay for favorable policy. This is not to say monarchies are immune to corruption, particularly among parliaments and courtiers, but the monarch himself has structurally the least reason of anyone in government to be corrupt.

### Being a monarch is a burden, not a privilege

Finally, the image of the monarch as a pampered figure of leisure misunderstands the role entirely. A king carries the security, reputation, and continuity of an entire nation on his shoulders, a duty that admits no retirement and no escape. As Sir Fulke Greville once put it: *"the greatest slave in a kingdom is generally the king of it."*
